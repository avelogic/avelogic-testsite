Payroll outsourcing vs in-house payroll: how Singapore SMEs should decide

Outsourced vs in-house payroll in Singapore: cost, compliance risk, confidentiality, PDPA data intermediary rules, and what stays with the employer either way.

By Avelogic Payroll & HR TeamUpdated 13 October 20266 min read

Key takeaways

  • Outsourcing moves the payroll work, but the legal responsibility for CPF, IRAS filing and personal data stays with you.
  • Under the PDPA, your payroll provider is a data intermediary. You need a written agreement setting out how it protects your data.
  • In-house payroll gives more control. Outsourcing gives specialist checking, cover for leave and resignations, and confidentiality.
  • A hybrid works well: outsource on the same system you could later run yourself.

Cost: compare the full picture

When you compare costs, include more than the payroll executive's salary. Add employer CPF and SDL on that salary, cover during their leave, training on rule changes, the time a manager spends checking their work, and the cost of mistakes, such as late CPF interest at 1.5% a month or penalties for late IRAS filing. For many companies with fewer than 50 staff, payroll is only part of one person's job, which makes outsourcing easier to justify.

What stays with you, whoever runs payroll

  • CPF: the employer must pay CPF by the 14th of the following month. Late payment interest is charged to the employer, not the provider.
  • IRAS: the employer is responsible for the accuracy of employee income submitted under the Auto-Inclusion Scheme by 1 March.
  • Payslips and records: MOM's itemised payslip and record-keeping duties sit with the employer.
  • Personal data: under the PDPA, the employer stays accountable for employee data handled by its provider.

The PDPA and your payroll provider

A payroll provider that processes personal data for you is a data intermediary. The PDPC's guidance makes three practical points:

  • You are ultimately responsible for setting the scope of the work and the security you require.
  • Not having a written contract that sets out the provider's key obligations is itself a PDPA breach.
  • You should require the provider to report data incidents promptly, and check its security through reports or audits.

Before signing, ask where your data is stored, who can access it, how it's encrypted, how logins are protected, and how incidents are reported.

When outsourcing makes most sense

  • Payroll falls to a director or office manager without payroll training.
  • You've had CPF, overtime or IRAS errors, or late payments.
  • Owners want salaries kept confidential from internal staff.
  • Your payroll person is leaving, or you need cover for long leave.

When in-house makes most sense

  • You have a trained HR or finance team with time for payroll.
  • Pay changes are frequent and need same-day turnaround, such as shift work with daily changes.
  • You want staff to manage leave, claims and attendance themselves in one connected system.

The hybrid approach

You don't have to choose for ever. If your provider runs payroll on software you can also license yourself, you can start outsourced and take payroll in-house later with all your history in place, or the reverse.

Frequently asked questions

If I outsource payroll, who is responsible for CPF?

The employer. The provider can calculate and prepare the CPF submission, but the employer must make sure CPF is paid by the 14th of the following month and bears any late payment interest.

Is a payroll provider covered by the PDPA?

Yes. A provider processing personal data on your behalf is a data intermediary under the PDPA, and you need a written agreement setting out its data protection obligations.

Is payroll outsourcing cheaper than hiring?

Often, for smaller companies, once you include employer CPF and SDL on a payroll salary, cover for leave, training and the cost of errors. Compare quotes against the full in-house cost.
